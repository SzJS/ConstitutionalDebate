# How Often Do Courts Overturn Wrong vs. Right Decisions? Reversal Rates, Error Rates, and What They Can and Cannot Tell Us Without Ground Truth

## TL;DR
- **There is no direct measurement of judicial error, because there is no ground truth; the numbers that exist fall into two families — observed reversal rates (heavily distorted by selection effects) and modeled error-rate estimates — and the single most defensible quantitative anchor is Gross et al.'s (PNAS, 2014) peer-reviewed estimate that "at least 4.1% would be exonerated... a conservative estimate of the proportion of false conviction among death sentences," converging with Risinger's independent 3.3–5% estimate for capital rape-murders.**
- **Reversal rates are NOT error rates: they range from under 1% of all convictions (England & Wales Crown Court) to ~71% (US Supreme Court merits cases) to 68% (Liebman's capital-case study), but each number is an artifact of which cases were appealed, the standard of review, and the reviewing court's own (possibly ideological, possibly erroneous) judgment — the Priest-Klein selection effect means litigated/appealed cases are a non-random, near-50/50 subset.**
- **The most rigorous lower bounds on error come not from reversals but from disagreement/noise studies (judge-jury agreement ~78%; SSA disability allowance rates varying by up to 46 points between judges; asylum grant rates from near 0% to over 90%) and from formal models (Shavell 1995; Kornhauser-Cameron) that treat appeals as a costly error-correction filter rather than a ground-truth oracle. For an AI-oversight analogy, the closest methodological relatives are latent-class / no-gold-standard diagnostic models (Hui-Walter 1980; Dawid-Skene 1979), which jointly estimate first-instance and reviewer error but rest on strong, often-violated independence assumptions.**

## Key Findings

1. **Observed reversal rates span two orders of magnitude and are dominated by selection.** US federal courts of appeals reverse roughly 5–20% of cases decided on the merits depending on case type and circuit; the US Supreme Court reverses ~71% of cases it hears — but only because it deliberately selects error-prone cases (a cert-selection artifact, not a base rate). England & Wales overturns under 1% of Crown Court convictions, but its administrative tribunals overturn 40–70% of the government decisions appealed to them.

2. **Administrative/benefits/immigration first-instance decisions have strikingly high overturn rates** — UK PIP disability appeals succeed ~66% of the time at a hearing, UK asylum appeals ~39–46%, US SSA disability ALJs vary enormously — which many read as direct evidence of high first-instance error, though selection and new-evidence effects complicate that reading.

3. **When researchers try to estimate actual innocence (ground truth), the numbers cluster at 3–5% for the most serious cases** (Gross 4.1%, Risinger 3.3–5%), with fierce methodological disputes pushing critics as low as ~0.016–0.062% (Cassell) and the observed exoneration rate at 1.6%.

4. **Inter-judge disagreement / "noise" studies provide the firmest lower bounds on error** because they need no ground truth: if identical cases get opposite outcomes from different judges, at least one is "wrong" by any consistency standard.

5. **Reversal is a poor proxy for error**, because appellate courts are themselves fallible and demonstrably ideological/panel-dependent; the theoretical literature (Shavell, Kornhauser-Cameron, Daughety-Reinganum) models appeal as a litigant-driven information-extraction device, not an error oracle.

## Details

### 1. Observed reversal / overturn rates (the "surface" statistics)

**US federal courts of appeals.** Reversal rates are computed only on cases "decided on the merits" (procedural terminations, often 50%+ of the docket in high-volume circuits, are excluded). For the 12-month period ending March 31, 2011, the 11th Circuit reversed 8.9% of merits cases overall — 5% of criminal cases, 11% of administrative appeals, and 26.1% of bankruptcy cases (the highest bankruptcy rate in the country that year), per Administrative Office Table B-5 data reported in the Florida Bar Journal. The Seventh Circuit had the highest overall rate that period (16.9%). More recent single-year figures (2022): private civil cases reverse most often (~13.4–13.6%), civil appeals involving the US ~10–12.5%, criminal appeals ~5–10%. Reversal in private civil cases has historically ranged 10–20% across circuits and case categories. The Eighth Circuit is a persistent low outlier (~5% overall, ~3% criminal). **Interpretation:** these are low single-to-low-double-digit numbers because most appeals fail; the vast majority of trial outcomes that are appealed are affirmed.

**US Supreme Court.** Since 2007 the Court has reversed roughly 71% of the cases it decides on the merits (average rate of reversal since 2007 is 71.4%). Per Ballotpedia's compilation of SCOTUSblog end-of-term data, the 2022 term decided 60 cases, reversing 42 (70%); the 2023 term decided 62 cases, "reversing a lower court in 44 (71 percent) of them"; and the October 2024 term reversed 47 of 67 (74.6%). By circuit since 2007, reversal rates run from the Ninth Circuit's ~79.4% down to the Seventh Circuit's ~63.2%. **Critical caveat — this ~71% is a selection artifact, not an error rate.** The Court grants certiorari to fewer than 1% of petitions, deliberately choosing cases it thinks are wrongly decided or that involve circuit splits. The reversal rate therefore measures the Court's cert-selection targeting, not the base error rate of the courts below. It is one of the most systematically misunderstood statistics in law.

**US state appellate courts.** Data are compiled by the National Center for State Courts' Court Statistics Project; state intermediate-appellate reversal rates are broadly comparable to (often somewhat higher than) federal rates, but coverage is uneven across states and mandatory-vs-discretionary jurisdiction structures differ, making cross-state comparison hazardous.

**England & Wales.** The system overturns a very small fraction of convictions. The Judicial Communications Office reported (for 2007/08) that "fewer than 1% of all convictions in the Crown Court and just 1.3% of sentences are successfully appealed," on a base of 1,541 conviction appeals and 5,313 sentence appeals received. More recent Court of Appeal (Criminal Division) sentence data: in 2023/24 the court received 2,875 sentencing-appeal applications and allowed 409 (14%), down from 672 of 3,356 (20%) in 2018–19 (Judiciary of England & Wales, *A Review of the Year in the Court of Appeal, Criminal Division, Oct 2023–Sep 2024*, published Jan 31, 2025). Appeals from magistrates' courts to the Crown Court succeed far more often: in 2024, 1,088 of 2,468 sentence appeals (44%) were allowed (MoJ, *Criminal Court Statistics Quarterly, Oct–Dec 2024*, Table C11), down from 50% in 2019.

**Criminal Cases Review Commission (CCRC).** The CCRC is the statutory backstop for suspected miscarriages of justice in England, Wales and Northern Ireland. From its 1997 start through Sept 30, 2017, it referred 634 of 21,780 closed cases back to the appeal courts — about 2.9% — and roughly 66–70% of referrals succeeded (convictions quashed or sentences reduced). Combining the two stages, the system overturns only ~2.5% of the cases presented to it as miscarriages, which critics characterize as the CCRC being excessively deferential to the Court of Appeal.

**Immigration/asylum and administrative tribunals (unusually high overturn rates).** These are the numbers most often cited as evidence of high first-instance error:
- **UK First-tier Tribunal (asylum/immigration):** ~39–46% of Home Office refusals appealed are overturned (46% Oct–Dec 2024; ~39% in 2025 as withdrawal rates rose). Taking appeals into account raised the overall asylum grant rate from 62% at initial decision to 70% after appeal for one cohort (Migration Observatory, 2025).
- **UK DWP disability benefits (PIP):** 66% of PIP appeals reaching a tribunal hearing were decided for the claimant in the latest quarter (HMCTS Tribunal Statistics, Q4 2024/25), a rate that has eased from ~70% in late 2023 to 64% in Oct–Dec 2025. DWP's own written parliamentary answer states "The PIP appeal overturn rate is 67%. It has remained stable around this rate since the second quarter of 2021/22" (UK Parliament written question 39207, 2025). Crucially, DWP's own management information (revealed in a 2022 parliamentary answer) indicated **91% of successful PIP appeals were won without any new written evidence** — in 59% of cases the tribunal reached a different conclusion on the same facts, in 32% the claimant gave effective oral evidence, and new written evidence made the difference in only 1% of cases — pointing to genuine first-instance error rather than new information.
- **US Social Security disability ALJs:** GAO-18-37 (published Dec 7, 2017) found "the allowance rate could vary by as much as 46 percentage points if different judges heard a typical claim (one that was average in all other factors GAO analyzed)"; individual FY2025 judge approval rates range from ~9% to ~93%.

**Selection effects and standard of review.** Every reversal number is filtered:
- Only appealed cases are observed, and appeals are filtered by cost and by the low probability of success.
- The **standard of review** mechanically drives reversal rates: de novo review (legal questions) produces more reversals than "clearly erroneous" (factual findings) or "abuse of discretion" (discretionary calls), which are highly deferential.
- The **Priest-Klein selection hypothesis** (Priest & Klein, "The Selection of Disputes for Litigation," *J. Legal Studies*, 1984) predicts that because parties settle cases with predictable outcomes, the disputes that actually go to trial/appeal cluster around the decision standard and trend toward a **50% plaintiff win rate**. The strong "50% limit" version holds only under specific assumptions (equal stakes, symmetric information); it has been formally proven under Priest-Klein's own assumptions (Klerman & Lee, *The Selection of Disputes at Forty*) but receives only modest empirical support, and asymmetric stakes shift the equilibrium away from 50%. The robust, near-universally-accepted core insight is that **litigated and appealed cases are a non-random, self-selected sample** — so reversal rates cannot be extrapolated to the full population of decisions.

### 2. Estimates of actual error rates (attempts at ground truth)

**Wrongful-conviction rate estimates.**
- **Risinger (2007), "Innocents Convicted"** (*J. Crim. L. & Criminology* 97:761): using DNA exonerations in capital rape-murders from 1982–1989 as a numerator and a 407-case sample as denominator, derived a **minimum factually wrongful conviction rate of 3.3%** with a "softer" ceiling of **~5%**. Notably framed against Justice Scalia's citation (in *Kansas v. Marsh*, 2006) of a 0.027% error rate — which Risinger's data directly refute.
- **Gross, O'Brien, Hu & Kennedy (2014)** (*PNAS* 111(20):7230, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1306417111): using survival analysis on 1973–2004 death sentences, estimated (verbatim) that "if all death-sentenced defendants remained under sentence of death indefinitely at least 4.1% would be exonerated. We conclude that this is a conservative estimate of the proportion of false conviction among death sentences in the United States." The observed exoneration rate was 1.6%; the gap arises because defendants removed from death row (resentenced to life) receive far less scrutiny, so their errors go undetected.
- **National Registry of Exonerations:** its 2024 Annual Report (released April 2025) records "3,646 exonerations in the United States from 1989 through the end of 2024," with 147 recorded for 2024 (141 in 28 states + 6 federal). Exonerees lost an average of 13.5 years each (nearly 1,980 years total in 2024); official misconduct featured in ~71% and perjury/false accusation in 72% (106 of 147) of 2024 cases. These are *counts*, not *rates* — they are a floor (only discovered errors), and are unrepresentative (concentrated in murder and drug cases, and in jurisdictions with active Conviction Integrity Units; e.g., 17 of Texas's 26 in 2024 stemmed from a single corrupt officer).
- **Criticisms of the headline figures.** The Gross 4.1% figure is contested on three grounds: (i) **exoneration ≠ factual innocence** — Gross uses exoneration as a proxy for innocence, which critics reject; (ii) **non-generalizability** — the 4.1% is derived from the atypically-scrutinized death-row subset (death-row exoneration rates run "orders of magnitude" and >130-to-1 higher than the general prison population), so it is arguably a capital-case ceiling, not a system-wide rate; (iii) **Paul Cassell** ("Overstating America's Wrongful Conviction Rate?", 60 *Ariz. L. Rev.* 815 (2018)) used a "component-parts" decomposition of plea vs. trial error to argue the violent-crime wrongful-conviction rate is **~0.016%–0.062%** (midpoint ~0.03%) — a range he says "comfortably embraces Justice Scalia's often-criticized figure" — three orders of magnitude below Gross. Cassell explicitly analyzes "factual innocence" or "actual innocence" (the "wrong person," or crime-that-never-happened cases), not "legal innocence," and warns "it is very easy for criminal justice critics to allege that an 'innocent' person has been convicted even when the facts strongly suggest otherwise." Gross's defenders respond that death-row scrutiny catches errors ordinary cases miss, making 4.1% a genuine lower bound for capital cases.

**The Liebman "Broken System" study.** Liebman, Fagan & West, *A Broken System: Error Rates in Capital Cases, 1973–1995* (Columbia, 2000), examined 4,578 state capital appeals and found an overall **68% rate of "serious reversible error"** across the three review stages (state direct appeal reversed 47%; federal habeas found serious error in 40% of what remained). Of reversals, 82% resulted in a lesser sentence on retrial and 9% (of reversals) in acquittal/not-guilty. **Criticisms:** (i) this is a *legal* error rate (reversible error), NOT a factual-innocence rate — a point even critics and supporters agree on; death-penalty proponents (e.g., Dudley Sharp) argue the "true" overturn-for-error rate is closer to ~30% or lower once law changes and reinstated sentences are excluded; (ii) methodologically, Liebman **excluded censored (not-yet-fully-reviewed) cases**, which statisticians (using self-consistent / inverse-probability-weighting estimators) noted can bias the reported 68%.

**Judge-jury agreement studies.** Kalven & Zeisel, *The American Jury* (1966), surveyed ~7,000 trials and found judges and juries agreed on the verdict in about **78%** of criminal cases (jury acquitted where judge would have convicted in ~19%). The same ~78% agreement held for civil liability (with disagreement roughly evenly split between plaintiff- and defense-favoring). Eisenberg, Hannaford-Agor, Hans et al. (2005), *Judge-Jury Agreement in Criminal Cases* (*J. Empirical Legal Studies* 2:171, a National Center for State Courts study), replicated this ~50 years later and found essentially the same agreement rate, concluding juries have a *higher* conviction threshold than judges. **What this does and does not measure:** a ~22% disagreement rate does not mean 22% error — the two adjudicators simply differ, and neither is ground truth — but it bounds *consistency*: at least one adjudicator is "wrong" in each disagreement under any single-correct-answer assumption.

**Inter-judge / inter-rater reliability ("random assignment" studies).** These provide the strongest ground-truth-free lower bounds on error:
- **Asylum ("Refugee Roulette"):** Ramji-Nogales, Schoenholtz & Schrag (60 *Stan. L. Rev.* 295 (2007)) analyzed ~133,000 asylum-officer decisions, ~140,000 immigration-judge decisions, ~126,000 BIA decisions and ~4,215 court-of-appeals decisions, finding grant rates from near **0% to over 90%** among adjudicators hearing similar caseloads — "the most important moment in an asylum case is the instant in which a clerk randomly assigns the application."
- **SSA disability:** GAO-18-37's up-to-46-percentage-point inter-judge spread (above).
- **Bail:** Kleinberg, Lakkaraju, Leskovec, Ludwig & Mullainathan, "Human Decisions and Machine Predictions" (*QJE* 133(1):237, 2018), exploited quasi-random assignment to judges and built an ML model of flight/re-arrest risk; a policy simulation showed **crime reductions up to 24.7% with no change in jailing rates, or jailing reductions up to 41.9% with no rise in crime** — implying substantial judicial misprediction (noise + bias). They carefully flag the **"selective labels"** problem: outcomes are observed only for released defendants, mirroring the ground-truth problem exactly.
- **Sentencing:** *Noise* (Kahneman, Sibony & Sunstein, 2021) synthesizes the sentencing-disparity literature (Judge Marvin Frankel's anecdotes; the 1974 Second Circuit study by Partridge & Eldridge; Anderson, Kling & Stith on the effect of the Federal Sentencing Guidelines), treating cross-judge sentence variation for identical cases as "system noise" — variability in judgments that should be identical. It documents, e.g., that expected inter-judge sentence differences fell from ~17% (4.9 months) in 1986–87 to ~11% (3.9 months) in 1988–93 after the Guidelines constrained discretion.

**Type II errors (false acquittals) and civil error.** These are far harder to detect than false convictions — there is no "exoneration" analog for a wrongly-acquitted defendant, so no comparable estimate exists; the Kalven-Zeisel finding that juries acquit in ~19% of cases where judges would convict is one of the few indirect windows. Civil "error" estimates are essentially nonexistent because civil disputes rarely have a verifiable ground truth; the Priest-Klein and judge-jury civil agreement data (~63–77% in smaller modern studies) are the closest proxies.

### 3. The meta-question: reversal ≠ error

**Appellate courts are themselves fallible and demonstrably ideological.** The strongest empirical body here is the panel-effects literature exploiting random assignment of three-judge federal panels:
- Sunstein, Schkade & Ellman, "Ideological Voting on Federal Courts of Appeals" (90 *Va. L. Rev.* 301 (2004)) and the book *Are Judges Political?* (Sunstein, Schkade, Ellman & Sawicki, Brookings 2006) found (a) judges' votes in contested areas are predictable from the appointing president's party ("party effects"); (b) these tendencies are amplified on same-party panels and dampened on mixed panels ("panel effects"). In many domains (affirmative action, sex discrimination, campaign finance, NLRB, environmental regulation) all three hypotheses were confirmed; in others (criminal appeals, federalism, takings) Republican and Democratic appointees voted essentially alike.
- Epstein, Landes & Posner, *The Behavior of Federal Judges* (2013), reach compatible conclusions modeling judges as (partly) ideological actors.

The implication for the user's question is sharp: **a reversal may reflect the reviewing panel's ideology or composition rather than genuine lower-court error.** Because assignment is random, the same trial decision could be affirmed or reversed depending on the luck of the panel draw — which means observed reversals contain irreducible "appellate noise."

**Error-correction vs. law-declaration functions.** Appellate review serves two distinct functions — correcting individual errors and declaring/harmonizing law — and reversals driven by the latter (e.g., resolving a circuit split, announcing a new rule) are not "error correction" of the lower court at all. This is why Supreme Court reversals in particular cannot be read as identifying lower-court "mistakes."

**Bayesian / no-gold-standard joint estimation — the part most relevant to AI oversight.** The methodologically closest tools to the user's problem come from **diagnostic-test evaluation without a gold standard**:
- **Hui-Walter (1980)** latent-class models jointly estimate the sensitivity/specificity of two imperfect tests using two populations of differing prevalence — with no ground truth — treating true status as latent.
- **Dawid-Skene (1979)** estimate rater error rates from multiple raters without a gold standard, assuming conditional independence of raters given the truth.
- **Critical limitation:** these models are only identified under strong assumptions (conditional independence of the "tests"/raters given the truth; two populations of differing prevalence). When those assumptions fail — as they generally do when raters share training, procedures, or biases — latent-class models systematically **overstate accuracy**; empirical comparisons against real gold standards show the true error rates are often larger than the model implies. This is directly analogous to the appeals problem: a trial court and appellate court are NOT conditionally independent "raters" (they see overlapping records, share legal culture, and the appellate court is influenced by the fact of appeal), so a naive latent-class estimate of their joint accuracy would be biased toward overconfidence. I found no published study that has successfully applied a fully-identified latent-class model to jointly estimate trial-court AND appellate-court error rates from reversal data — this appears to be a genuine gap, and the reason is precisely the independence-violation problem. (A 2026 arXiv working paper, "Partial Identification from LLM Prompts," makes exactly this point for correlated LLM raters and turns to *partial* identification/bounds when Dawid-Skene independence is unavailable — a template worth borrowing.)

### 4. Formal models of hierarchical review as error correction

- **Shavell, "The Appeals Process as a Means of Error Correction"** (*J. Legal Studies* 24:379 (1995)) is the foundational model. Its core result: the appeals process lets society "harness information that litigants have about erroneous decisions and thereby reduce the incidence of mistake at low cost, because the appeals tribunal convenes only in a subset of cases." This explains why appeal can be cheaper and better than simply improving trial quality (you only re-examine the cases litigants flag as probably-erroneous), and why the *right to appeal is given to disappointed litigants* rather than to the higher court's own initiative. Shavell extended this in "The Appeals Process and Adjudicator Incentives" (2006) and "On the Design of the Appeals Process" (2010, on discretionary vs. direct review). Edward Schwartz's comment (and Shavell's reply) debate whether appellate judges should draw inferences from the mere fact that an appeal was brought.
- **Kornhauser & Cameron** developed the "team" vs. "principal-agent" framing of judicial hierarchy. Kornhauser (1995) gives an informal *team model* (all judges share the goal of minimizing errors; hierarchy exists to promote accuracy). The *political/principal-agent model* (e.g., Songer, Segal & Cameron, "The Hierarchy of Justice," *AJPS* 38:673 (1994)) instead treats lower courts as agents whom a higher-court principal must monitor; that study found circuit courts highly responsive to Supreme Court search-and-seizure policy but also retaining strong independent preferences. Cameron & Kornhauser, "Decision Rules in a Judicial Hierarchy" (2005) and "Appeals Mechanisms, Litigant Selection, and the Structure of Judicial Hierarchies" (2006) show that a three-tier hierarchy (trial / intermediate / supreme) can under specified conditions drive the error rate to zero — but, once litigants may be asymmetrically informed, only under restrictive conditions, and the optimal rule sometimes requires a lower court to decide *against* the informed litigant in the absence of a clear signal, exploiting that litigant's private knowledge (i.e., forcing information revelation through appeal incentives).
- **Daughety & Reinganum** and related law-and-economics work model appeal and settlement bargaining jointly under incomplete information. Roussey & Soubeyran ("Does the Appeals Process Reduce the Occurrence of Legal Errors?", 2018) and Hylton & Kim ("The Economics of Appeals," *J. Law & Econ.* 2026) extend the tradition.

**The unifying theoretical lesson for AI oversight:** in all these models the appeals mechanism does not directly observe truth; it works by giving the party with private information about an error a costly channel to force reconsideration. Its error-correction power depends on (a) litigants' information about errors, (b) the cost filter that keeps volume low, and (c) the reviewer being more accurate than the first instance. If any of these fail — reviewers no better than first instance, cost filter mis-calibrated, or informed parties unable/unwilling to appeal — the mechanism degrades, and observed reversal rates become uninformative about true error.

## Recommendations

**For using these numbers as an AI-oversight / scalable-oversight analogy:**

1. **Do not treat any reversal rate as an error rate.** Use them only as what they are — the joint output of a selection process and a (fallible) reviewer. The ~71% SCOTUS figure in particular should be flagged in any write-up as a cert-selection artifact, not a base rate. The right first-instance-error anchors are the modeled innocence estimates (3.3–5% for the most serious criminal cases) and, for a broad "inconsistency floor," the ~22% judge-jury disagreement rate.

2. **Prioritize the noise/random-assignment paradigm as your primary analogy.** Kleinberg et al. (bail) and Refugee Roulette (asylum) are the cleanest templates: random assignment of cases to decision-makers converts unobservable "error" into observable *disagreement*, which lower-bounds error without ground truth. This maps directly onto AI-oversight designs where you route identical inputs to multiple overseers and measure divergence. The "selective labels" problem Kleinberg names is exactly the ground-truth problem you flagged.

3. **Adopt the no-gold-standard diagnostic-testing framework (Hui-Walter, Dawid-Skene) as the formal backbone — but budget for its failure mode.** These models are the mathematically correct way to jointly estimate first-instance and reviewer accuracy from agreement data alone. The binding constraint is the conditional-independence assumption; because trial and appellate courts (like an AI and its AI overseer trained on shared data) are correlated raters, naive application will overstate accuracy. Any AI-oversight estimator should build in dependence corrections or move to *partial* identification / bounds (as the 2026 LLM-prompts paper does).

4. **Read Shavell (1995) and Cameron-Kornhauser (2005/2006) as the design theory.** They tell you the conditions under which a cheap, litigant-triggered review layer improves accuracy — precisely the question in scalable oversight (when does a cheaper/narrower checker improve a system without observing truth?).

**Benchmarks/thresholds that would change these conclusions:**
- If a *fully-identified* latent-class estimate of joint trial+appellate error from reversal data were published (closing the Section 3 gap), it would supersede the indirect estimates here — watch the empirical-legal-studies and applied-statistics literatures for it.
- If reversal rates in a domain approach 50% (as in some tribunals), Priest-Klein implies the litigated cases are maximally uncertain and the *first-instance* decision on that subset is close to a coin flip — a signal to check whether first-instance quality, not just selection, is the problem (the DWP "same evidence" finding is the strongest such signal).
- A domain where reviewers are demonstrably no more accurate than first-instance deciders (e.g., DWP Mandatory Reconsideration by the same agency changes only ~20–25% of decisions vs. ~66% overturned at the independent tribunal) is one where a same-organization "oversight" layer adds little — a directly transferable warning for AI systems that use same-model self-review versus independent review.

## Caveats

- **No ground truth, by construction.** Every "error rate" in this report is either a discovered-error floor (exonerations), a modeled extrapolation (Gross, Risinger, Liebman), or a consistency/noise measure (judge-jury, inter-judge). None measures true error directly; your premise is correct and unavoidable.
- **Reversal ≠ error, and error ≠ injustice.** A legally correct reversal can free a factually guilty person; a factually accurate conviction can be reversed on procedural grounds. The Liebman 68% is *legal* reversible error; the Gross 4.1% is *factual* innocence; these are different quantities and must not be blended.
- **Contested headline numbers.** The Gross 4.1% (critics: exoneration≠innocence, non-generalizable, Cassell's ~0.03%) and the Liebman 68% (critics: censoring bias, legal-not-factual error, ~30% alternative) are the two most disputed figures and are presented with their critiques above.
- **Source quality varies.** Official statistics (Administrative Office of US Courts, GAO, MoJ/HMCTS, National Registry of Exonerations) and peer-reviewed studies (PNAS, QJE, *J. Empirical Legal Studies*, *J. Legal Studies*, *Stanford/Virginia Law Review*) are weighted most heavily. Some UK tribunal success-rate figures were read via reputable secondary compilers (Sentencing Academy, Migration Observatory, Disability Rights UK) citing primary MoJ/DWP data; the exact current CACD conviction-appeal (as opposed to sentence-appeal) allowed-percentage was not pinned to a single primary table and should be confirmed directly from MoJ Criminal Court Statistics if a precise figure is needed.
- **Selection pervades everything.** Because appeals are voluntary, costly, and filtered by predicted success, observed pools are non-random. This is not a minor caveat — it is the central reason the surface statistics cannot answer your question directly, and why the modeling and noise literatures are the substantive answer.
- **Forward-looking modeling results are conditional.** The Kleinberg 24.7%/41.9% figures and the Cameron-Kornhauser "error rate to zero" results are model outputs under stated assumptions (unbiased counterfactuals; specified information structures), not observed real-world reductions; they should be cited as such.
